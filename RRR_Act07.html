Act 7

Marking Time and Percussive Corporeality

by Alexis McGee

transcript

 

Director’s Note

  • Embodied Rhetorics: Recall that our “Introduction” cites Happe, who argues that racialized bodies are reproduced through supposedly neutral norms, which are so deeply codified that they normalize racism as an everyday cultural performance (133). This Act stages reiterations of embodied communication—specifically “Stepping”—that function as acts of agency, demonstrating “resistance, liberation, and joy through our bodies and beyond just words” (McGee).
  • Performing CDA: Inverting CDA’s top-down approach to analysis, this Act emphatically inserts the Black body as methodology in a fully embodied grassroots approach to linguistic critique. In doing so, it celebrates Black expression as a re-shaping of social power.
  • Boal Connection: Note how this Act interweaves Boal’s stages Two through Four in “Poetics of the Oppressed” (Introduction) to create a palimpsest—one messy, simultaneous event, with layers of new and old meaning emerging at once rather than in progression. This too recalls Ingold's meshwork in our “Introduction.” The body performs a fusion of Boal’s stages, becoming a rhythmic archive of Black history and identity.

Artist Statement

Assemblages are inherently productive, entering into polyvalent becomings to produce and give expression to previously nonexistent realities, thoughts, bodies, affects, spaces, actions, ideas, and so on.—Weheliye, Habeas Viscus: Racializing Assemblages, Biopolitics, and Black Feminist Theories of the Human.

This assemblage of footage and mixed media plays on a much older artistic and rhetorical tradition: that of the palimpsest. A palimpsest is a layering technique where written work is continually built upon with “newer,” revised, or all-together different compositions while the older, “original” work remains visible. The opening words, which serve as an origin point for this artistic work, are layered and entwined with one another. The collage of layered media presented here (the paragraphs and quotes taken from this artist statement, as well as the collection of images, bodies, sounds, and narrations through camera lenses, audio clips, and cellphone footage is assembled or collected in the video/essay) is meant to replicate the palimpsest showcasing the important, interconnected, and multidimensional network of stepping’s marking of time and percussive corporeality.

Rehearse, Revise, Regenerate draws on long and storied traditions of moments where the body intersects, reflects, and performs language—both everyday commonplace experiences and interpretations of being recognized (or not) in the world. More specifically, the work of Augusto Boal frames the interrogations of body and language found in Rehearse, Revise, Regenerate. This chapter, therefore, extends such networked conversations by re/presenting Boal’s stages of poetics (as discussed in the Introduction of this book) within and through the body, demonstrating Boal’s stages two, three, four as a blurry, messy, inter-and intra-personal communicative event. As Harris Ramsby and LaFrance explain in the “Introduction,”

Boal’s "stage two” entailed “Making the body expressive,” suggesting “a series of games by which one begins to express one’s self through the body, abandoning other, more common and habitual forms of expression.” Stage three, “Theatre as language,” calls for practicing “theater as a language that is living and present, not as a finished product displaying images from the past,” while stage four requires “spect-actors” to understand “spectacles” according to their need to discuss certain themes or rehearse certain actions. (2-3)

When read through Black rhetorical and performative traditions (like Stepping, a form of Black dance), these stages can culminate in a dynamic percussive corporeality that documents and speaks to our past, present, and future. Stepping, for instance, not only centers the body as the main actor/rhetor, but also requires the body to communicate not through words, per se, but by simultaneously theorizing one’s affordances of play, emotion, and identity. Within this Act’s palimpsest of scenes, media, and conversations, Boal’s stages are reflected as layered experiences that help us think through how Black ways of knowing are informed by the body and help us better understand how language that is constructed outside of and/or in resistance to normative, linear understandings of body, language, and politics can be interpreted within a situated sociocultural and historical reality.

Rhetoricians and Black Studies scholars alike have taken up the body as a theoretical and physical medium in which to necessitate recognition of the body’s humanity and the social conditions imposed upon one’s agency-making abilities. More importantly, the body is the centralizing text that has the ability to inform relationships of meaning through specific kinds of sociocultural and symbolic language. While Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA) can view relationships of meaning from the top down (van Dijk), this work spotlights how CDA can be implemented from the ground level up to re/address how grassroots movements inform higher social powers—like the influence of international superstar Beyoncé. Rather than the fricatives and glottal stops (for instance) that can be quantified and analyzed in Linguistics (types of data that drive contemporary CDA approaches), this chapter investigates other bodily phenomena that act as stand-ins for the more normative approaches to language and communication. As Harris Ramsby and LaFrance note in the “Introduction,” “The attraction to CDA for many scholars in linguistics and rhetoric stems from the bridges it enables between everyday language practice and larger social economies” (5). That may be so, but this Act’s use of CDA intentionally complicates these bridges to re/insert the body in its methodological approach. To this end, this Act uses CDA’s approach to recognizing media as a social process/practice that conveys and transfers meaning as well as temporality as a kind of discourse frame to “address social problems [and] understand power relations as discursive” (Harris Ramsby and LaFrance, “Introduction” 5). In other words, the CDA approaches recognized here validate the body’s ability to communicate and amplify the ways inequities are systemically imposed onto marginalized communities and the resultant ways those communities can speak back to multiple forms of oppression like the over-reliance, or assumption of dependency, on written and verbal language (namely Standard White English).

The body, especially for many Black people, has its own way of languaging time and meaning—connecting time to our lived realities in ways that simultaneously express urgency and give space for reflection. Our arms, hands, mouth, feet, chest, tongue, thighs re/produce messages that harken back to specific times and important social movements, confront past histories, and reconcile additional layered meanings into our daily existence. The adornment of the body, too, our hair, clothes, shoes, etc. serves as part of the corporeal rhetorical network dictating time as part of our narrative. Our bodies are never neutral; they can be text, discourse, and social practice. Each stomp, clap, chest thump, grunt, suicide fall, and “mean-muggin” face is an embodied and performative paralexical “word” that means something: “Definitionally, parlexical refers to the things that are adjacent to lexicon and grammar; things like sounds (such as beatboxing) that are not quite words, but are still a part of communicating meaning within a sentence or specific moment of interpersonal communication. . . [Paralexical “words”] are concocted words that create and replicate meaning radiating from the bodily experience or emotion” (McGee 126-27). Through this interpretation of language, we can understand step (dancing) as a performative venue for communication. Black folks have communicated resistance, liberation, and joy through our bodies and beyond just words, which, in turn, asserts our agency and temporality (or long-established existence) as being more than capable of not only surviving but thriving at times in oppressive environments. The clips of Black bodies stepping on a stage at a concert, at a probate on campus, and in the newsroom showcase the body’s ability to work media and affix gazes upon the body en/forcing spectators to see us “take up space” in meaningful, resistive ways.

This work aims to highlight the relationship of temporality and corporeality while centering Black rhetorics and ways of thinking. This work’s primary focus isn’t about highlighting the different attitudes, philosophies, or histories of Black women’s social clubs and their contemporary iterations of Greek life. It isn’t about self-promotion or competition. I am wanting to tie together the rhetorical sense of temporality (as it is communicated by/through stepping) as a means of claiming agency in and through the body (with and without the traditional understanding of “words”). The artistic work is meant to illuminate how Black rhetorics weave a network of resistance through establishing a community, engaging in call-and-response, and cultivating spaces to be unapologetically Black in times where Black bodies are often de/valued via assemblage. In simpler terms, this work is intended to demonstrate how types of public media can act or reverberate through the body and rhetorically signal the need for social action—be that as critique or celebration, camaraderie or competition, care and consideration.

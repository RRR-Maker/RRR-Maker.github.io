Act 5

Un-M-Othered
by Liz DeBetta

transcript

 

Artist Statement

Rhetorical, CDA, and Theater Connections:

  • Rhetorical framing: Discourse dependency, master narratives, power through language
  • CDA connection: Metaphor theory, presupposition, subject-object constructions
  • Boal connections: Making the Body Expressive, Theatre as Language, Theatre as Discourse

As a graduate student, one of the first seminars I had to take was “Critical Writing and Thinking: Analysis and Synthesis as Inquiry. The final project for the course was to write a critical discourse analysis that built upon previous metaphorical and multimodal analyses I had written. What I ended up writing, in those early days of my exploration into adoption narratives and their impact, was “The Language of Adoption: Narratives of Displacement and Deception, a Discourse Analysis.” This turned out to be a foundational piece of writing and thinking for me, and is one that I returned to when approached to contribute to this collection. It is also what helped me plant the seeds of what was to become Un-M-Othered: A Story of Adoption and Patriarchy, which is my own award-winning solo performance piece that, in the spirit of this collection, is positioned 100% at the intersections of performance, critical rhetoric, and language play.

Diving into the language of adoption as it exists in the public discourse was not only fascinating, it was also infuriating. I began to compare the many feel-good stories that the American public loves to consume about adoption against my own internal experiences, and those of other adoptees I was in dialogue with. The more I read and analyzed what the media put forth (and wants us to swallow wholesale) the more I realized I needed to do something to push back against the dominant narratives that obfuscate and sugar coat the truth of maternal separation as complex early trauma that has debilitating effects over time (Knox et al. 2021). I needed to de-center those dominant narratives and replace them with the truth of lived experience by performing the metaphors that had once displaced and deceived me. Metaphors that once internalized become difficult to displace, like “you were our gift from God ” - bolstered by statements like I should “be grateful”, that I “was so lucky” to be adopted by people who loved me, people that “wanted” me and had “chosen” me.

My work, as a writer and performer, became firmly rooted in my identity as a woman and an adoptee. And from this location I began to rewrite myself into being, by rewriting the story I had been told my whole life. I did the work of what I now call “re-narrativizing” by first writing and then speaking aloud what had once been forced into silence (DeBetta Adult Adoptees, 2023)

Subject-Object Power & Critical Language Play

Un-M-Othered grew out of a desire to use poetic language and personal narrative to rhetorically reconstruct myself and my story in order to shine a light on the deeply entrenched patriarchal system of oppression that is the culture of adoption (DeBetta Un-M-Othered, 2020). Writing, and then staging the piece, was a full-blown exercise in becoming the subject instead of the object in my story in order to problematize and reconstruct the story that had been told to me but not by me. Like Boal, Deirdre Heddon, in her book Autobiography and Performance, is concerned with using performance to make visible the dominant, and often incorrect, assumptions about particular subjects,

The autobiographical and the political are interconnected. Who speaks? What is spoken? What sorts of lives are represented, contested, imagined? The vast majority of autobiographical performances have been concerned with using the public arena of performance in order to ‘speak out’, attempting to make visible denied or marginalized subjects, or to ‘talk back’, aiming to challenge, contest, and problematize dominant representations and assumptions about those subjects. (20)

My story, like that of so many other adopted people, had been co-opted by a culture of silence and shame. I had been socially conditioned not to speak, not to question, and not to even think about who I had been before I was told who I should be. Heddon’s questions, positioned alongside Susan Gair and Sharon Moloney’s “unspeakable stories” or “counter-narratives” granted me permission to speak freely and openly to contest the story that had been handed to me and instead tell a story that has not (yet) been deemed a socially acceptable and palatable form of narrative around adoption. Thus, Un-M-Othered became a way for me as the writer and performer to create space for audiences to imagine the complexity and pain of adoption as it exists for me, and many other adopted people, and also to consider the tragedy of loss that occurs for first mothers and their children while simultaneously fighting back against the oppression of silence by speaking truth to power (51). In fact, the title itself is a rhetorical representation of the subject-object power, oppression, and critical language play necessary to unpack and recognize the way master narratives disenfranchise those of us affected by adoption. The dashes represent the disruption and disconnection caused by maternal separation and highlight the separation that exists between adoptees and first mothers/families and also their adoptive families. Adoption makes un-mothers of first mothers and adoptive mothers are also un-mothered by infertility (one of the most frequent reasons many choose to adopt). And finally, as women we are all “othered” in the process of adoption and its intersection with patriarchy. Thus the title works to rhetorically speak to multiple oppressions and the need to reconstruct language and story.!

The emotional glue of this piece is the poetry – it allows me to play with words and images with an immediacy that other forms don’t. Poetry allows me to use metaphor and turn it on itself in order to revise and rethink the truth. It allows me to embody the pain and transform it. It allows me to be seen in the most vulnerable way possible because the poems in this piece are, and always have been, the truth I have carried in my bones. And this truth, once witnessed, cannot be unsaid, unheard, or unknown.

Critical Metaphor Theory, Presupposition Analysis & Theatre of the Oppressed

“I’m supposed to be a gift, a blessing, God’s plan, an answer to a prayer…I swallow myself in order to keep the illusion.”  This line from Un-M-Othered embodies the very premise of this act: to root out the iniquitous language practices embedded in adoption discourse, and to reclaim agency through rhetorical performance. My work explores how dominant metaphors—such as being “chosen,” “a blessing,” or “a gift”—reframe adoptees as commodities and erase the trauma of maternal separation. Using critical metaphor theory, I deconstruct how these phrases become internalized, policing adoptee expression and reinforcing silence. I employ subject-object constructions and presupposition analysisto surface the invisible but insidious ways in which adoption discourse positions adoptees as grateful recipients and renders birth mothers absent. For example, the line “I swallow myself in order to keep the illusion” (00:00:44) enacts subject-object reversal, as I simultaneously become both agent and object, demonstrating the internalization of dominant narratives that demand silence to maintain the socially acceptable image of adoption. Similarly, the statement “My life made possible by the pain of separation” (00:02:00) exposes a buried presupposition: that my existence in my adoptive context is contingent upon an erasure—a foundational loss masked by euphemism. These are not neutral phrases—they are rhetorical acts of erasure that contribute to the social normalization of trauma. Drawing on Boal’s Theatre of the Oppressed, particularly the stages of “Making the Body Expressive” and “Theatre as Discourse,” I use solo performance to embody counter-narratives. I stage the embodied consequences of being rendered voiceless. In this act, I become the subject of my story—not its object—by revising the script I was given and performing the truth that lives in my bones.

This act builds directly on Critical Discursive Rhetorical Performance (CDRP) by transforming theoretical CDA into lived critique. The work exposes how language is inscribed onto and through bodies, and how performance can make that inscription visible—and therefore contestable. In the words of Boal, this is not theater as spectacle, but as transformation. It is a rhetorical strategy that asks: What does it mean to be seen, not as a symbol of gratitude or resolution, but as a human being with voice, history, and truth?

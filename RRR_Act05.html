Un-M-Othered

Liz DeBetta

Director’s Note

  • Embodied Rhetorics: Notice in both text and video how this Act describes and enacts the body as both inheriting and undoing dominant discourses of adoption.
  • Performing CDA: Look for how the performance incorporates CDA abstractions into monologue and movement that viewers can actually witness—metaphor, subject-object fusion, and presupposition, each one drawn from the dominant discourses of adoption that Liz is working to dismantle, to make contestable.
  • Boal Connection: Watch how DeBetta’s body moves to assume the discourses of adoption. With her theatrical performance background, she effortlessly inhabits the classic Boal suggestion to “[Make] the Body Expressive.” Coupled with her recognition of the language that CDA unearths, her postures and gestures surface her emotions and intentions, which fuse with the narrative.

Artist Statement

As a graduate student, one of the first seminars I took was “Critical Writing and Thinking: Analysis and Synthesis as Inquiry.” The final project for the course was to write a critical discourse analysis that built upon my previous metaphorical and multimodal analyses around discourses of adoption. This helped me plant the seeds of what was to become Un-M-Othered: A Story of Adoption and Patriarchy, my solo performance piece that, in the spirit of this collection, is positioned at the intersections of performance, critical rhetoric, and language play. My video excerpts a few minutes from this full-length theatrical one-woman show, particularly those moments that enact dominant metaphors of adoption, subject-object constructions, and presupposition as CDRP principles.

Un-M-Othered grew out of a desire to use poetic language and personal narrative to rhetorically reconstruct myself and my story in order to shine a light on the deeply entrenched patriarchal system of oppression that is the culture of adoption (DeBetta, Adult Adoptees). Adoption makes un-mothers of first mothers, and adoptive mothers are also un-mothered by infertility (one of the most frequent reasons many choose to adopt). Thus, the title works rhetorically to speak for the need to reconstruct discourses of adoption.

Diving into the language of adoption as it exists in the public discourse was not only fascinating; it was also infuriating. In researching for Un-M-Othered, I compared the many feel-good stories that the American public consumes about adoption against my own internal experiences and those of other adoptees I was in dialogue with. The more I read and analyzed what the media put forth, the more I realized I needed to do something to push back against the dominant narratives that obfuscate and sugarcoat the truth of maternal separation as complex early trauma that has debilitating effects on adoptees over time (Knox et al. 766505).

CDRP in Un-M-Othered

The excerpt I share disrupts dominant narratives about adoption and replaces them with the truth of lived experience. In the video, I twice perform the metaphors that establish my adoption story. Indeed, the language of adoption frequently describes adopted children as “a gift,” “a blessing,” “God’s plan,” or “an answer to a prayer.” My video explores how these dominant metaphors—such as being “chosen,” “a blessing,” or “a gift”—package adoptees as commodities and erases the trauma of maternal separation (0:36, 0:51 & 1:40). Once internalized, these metaphorical mappings become difficult to displace. In my performance, I deconstruct how these phrases become internalized, policing adoptee expression and reinforcing silence.

I also enact subject-object constructions and perform presupposition analysis to surface the invisible ways in which adoption discourse positions adoptees as grateful recipients and renders birth mothers absent. For example, the line “I swallow myself in order to keep the illusion” (0:44) enacts subject-object fusion, as I simultaneously become both agent and object, demonstrating the internalization of dominant narratives that demand silence to maintain the socially acceptable image of adoption.

The statement “My life made possible by the pain of separation” (2:00) exposes a buried presupposition: that my existence in my adoptive context is contingent upon the erasure of my birth mother and the circumstances that led to my adoption. This presuppositional content survives across different contexts—whether the adoption story is traumatic or empowering, the underlying assumption about necessary trade-offs remains intact. In my experience, these are not neutral phrases—they are rhetorical acts of erasure that contribute to the social normalization of adoption trauma.

Drawing on Boal’s Theatre of the Oppressed, particularly the stages of “Making the Body Expressive” and “Theatre as Discourse,” I use solo performance to embody counter-narratives. I stage the embodied consequences of being rendered voiceless. In this act, I become the subject of my story—not its object—by revising the script I was given and performing the truth that lives in my bones.

This act builds directly on CDRP by transforming theoretical critical discourse analysis into lived critique. The work exposes how language is inscribed onto and through bodies and how performance can make that inscription visible—and therefore contestable. In the spirit of Boal, this is not theater as spectacle but as transformation. It is a rhetorical strategy that asks, “What does it mean to be seen, not as a symbol of gratitude or resolution, but as a human being with voice, history, and truth?”

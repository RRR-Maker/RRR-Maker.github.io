Act 1

The Doorway
by Lydia McDermott

transcript

 

Artist Statement

Rhetorical, CDA, and Theater Connections:

  • Rhetorical framing: Embodied rhetoric, rhetorics of place, disability rhetorics
  • CDA connection: Syntax and pragmatics
  • Boal connections: Rituals and masks; Newspaper theater

This video began as a much shorter video created by my student, Sueli Gwiazdowski, as a final assignment for a course called “Rhetorical Bodies.” She called it “Normate Doorway.” The assignment was to intervene in some way in bodily rhetorics-as-normal. Earlier in the semester, the class had performed a “reading” of a space on campus that communicated power relations that they were called upon to embody. That earlier assignment led to Sueli’s final video assignment: Sueli hoped to illustrate the bodily rhetoric of a wheelchair user faced with an inaccessible doorway – a structure designed to enforce a power dynamic that denied wheelchair users access entirely, thereby stripping them of power. Soon after the course ended, and Sueli had turned in her project, I attended the RSA summer workshop that spawned this collection. Within the workshop, I worked on completely different material, but the more I learned and practiced, the more I kept coming back to the germ of an idea in Sueli’s project. In the following year, Sueli and I worked on two independent studies on disability rhetorics, and within that time, we discussed how CDA might apply to the video that Sueli created, and how it might be re-created with CDA explicitly in focus. And so, we began filming approaches to the doorway and recording audio for me later to put together into this final video, “The Doorway.”

We took the original “Normate Doorway” video and expanded upon it, filming additional approaches to the same door from various angles, including a first-person point of view, and documenting access via the ramp that leads to the door. The first-person approach was inspired by Augusto Boal’s “Rituals and Masks,” a technique that analyzes and reshapes social rituals by altering the roles and positions of participants. By positioning the viewer to experience the vantage point of a wheelchair user, we aimed to transform the typical perspective on accessibility. I also spent some time interviewing Sueli about her experiences as a student with a wheelchair on our campus, and specifically about the incident when her wheelchair was stolen.

Because we also wanted CDA to be central to the video, we included the “accessibility statement” found on our college’s website as text being typed across the video. The statement in its entirety is:

Whitman College is committed to ensuring accessibility for all members of our community. We aim to provide an accessible experience where all individuals can access programs, opportunities, and activities. Additionally, Whitman is committed to promoting access and awareness as a resource to all community members. We are committed to ensuring that all persons in our community and all visitors to our campus feel welcome and included and that appropriate accessibility accommodations have been provided for those who require this service. (“Accessibility at Whitman,” emphasis added)

Not only does the video’s portrayal of Sueli’s struggle with an inaccessible door ironically highlight the disconnect between the statement and the lived reality on campus, but also the verb constructions are declarative and passive, suggesting a factual relationship to lived reality. When the constructions are not declarative, they are optative, expressing an ideal or wish toward which to strive, which additionally contrasts with the declarative nature of the commitment expressed in the opening lines. Of course, the concept of “accessibility” is already a nominalization of the verb we hope for: to access.

In the fashion of Boal’s newspaper theatre (121-2), I also incorporated an excerpt from a student newspaper that Sueli had found in her research for another project. This historic quote comes from a letter to the editor by a student wheelchair user and echoes the same processes we are watching on screen, reinforcing the interview excerpts playing in audio over the film clip, illustrating the static condition of accessibility on campus.

In addition, I have included two central quotes from a text Sueli and I read together, Jay Dolmage’s Academic Ableism. First, to ground our CDRP work in rhetoric, we took Dolmage’s definition of rhetoric and his explanation of its relevance to the built environments through which we move:

If rhetoric is the circulation of discourse through the body, then spaces and institutions cannot be disconnected from the bodies within them, the bodies they selectively exclude, and the bodies that actively intervene to reshape them. (9)

Our video literally portrays this process of intervention a wheelchair user must employ in order to gain the access the building, i. e. institution, wants to deny them. Later we include the following quote:

When accommodations that students with disabilities have access to, over and over again, are intended to simply temporarily even the playing field for them in a single class or activity, it is clear that these retrofits are not designed for people to live and thrive with a disability, but rather to temporarily make the disability go away. (70)

Accommodations are the exception that prove the rule that people with disabilities are not intended to be on our campuses. This video refuses to accept that rule.

Act 2

Dirt: A Play in Composted Texts

Michelle La France

transcript

 

Director’s Note

  • Embodied Rhetorics: This Act dramatizes ambient and material rhetorics, giving voice to soil, dirt, and compost as entangled, non-neutral participants in an eco-consciousness rather than inert backdrop to human activity.
  • Performing CDA: As you watch the video below and read the Artist’s Statement, notice how this piece invites you to think through the metaphors embedded in everyday language. Pay particular attention to how common definitions, here “dirt” and “soil,” quietly encode cultural value judgments. Through metaphor, emphasis, and framing, definitions embed value systems into our perceptions of the natural world.
  • Boal Connection: Adopting Boal’s concept of the “spectator-actor” (134), which reimagines passive audience members as active participants in their own lives, this Act channels Boal’s affinity for found texts as the source of theatrical intervention. Through the playful reassembly of online definitional texts, the script engages layers of meaning buried within familiar language, inviting audiences to recognize and reconsider the ideologies embedded within everyday discourse.

Artist Statement

Despite all our accomplishments, we owe our existence to a six-inch layer of topsoil and the fact that it rains.—Paul Harvey, “So God Made a Farmer”

This short farcical film, a play for four voice actors, is an absurdist experiment with excerpted definitions of dirt, soil, and compost. The video shows a garden, a compost pile, a person turning a compost pile, and a small forest/mushroom farm, while disembodied voices read found texts copied directly from online dictionaries and public comments on gardening websites. The thesis from an online academic essay, a cognitive linguist, and a few lines from Sharon Old’s poem “Ode to Dirt” are also included.

Here, the Dirt, Soil, and Compost found in gardening texts become dueling protagonists who read their own definitions, while the Stage Director narrates a series of comedic interactions between those characters. By isolating terms from their lived contexts, definitions condense meaning into seemingly neutral frameworks that actually reflect and reinforce dominant ideologies.

The script juxtaposes definitions of dirt, soil, and compost to problematize cultural reactions to “dirt” as an entity. The Cambridge English Dictionary defines dirt as “dust, soil, or any substance that makes a surface not clean.” In contrast, the same dictionary defines soil as “the material on the surface of the ground in which plants grow” and compost as “decaying plant material that is added to soil to improve its quality.” While discussions among gardeners occasionally acknowledge dirt’s ecological importance, public discourse tends to see dirt as a passive or lifeless object to be removed or cleaned; soil is more typically defined as a living and rich medium (that includes lifeless dirt) and compost as a human-driven process that improves soil health.

Lizzardo’s metaphorical analysis underscores a cultural unease with dirt. He writes, “much of our conceptualization and reasoning about moral propriety is framed by a set of metaphors that originate from a conceptual structure generated from our experiences with dirt and cleanliness.” Our definitions of the natural world do not simply describe; they participate in broader ideological systems that naturalize hierarchies and exclusions. These distinctions symbolically and rhetorically impose a rigid binary understanding upon complex systems.

Thomas Rickert’s Ambient Rhetoric has troubled similar “subject-object” boundaries central to western rhetoric, claiming these conceptualizations have reified human exceptionalism and erased the agency of the natural world. Rickert writes:

World may be world for human beings, insofar as it is exceedingly difficult to extricate ourselves from the world that already takes part in who and how we are, but it does not depend on us, for it emerges from everything we do amid everything else that is. (221-222)

Rickert’s critique emphasizes that language and meaning emerge through entanglements with the more-than-human world. Adopting Rickert’s ecological stance, we might argue that understanding “dirt” as purely “lifeless” reinforces rhetoric that privileges human agency, obscuring the potentials of nonhuman forces and reifying human exceptionalism.

It is worth noting that Prasad and Maraj have critiqued Rickert and other “post-human” and environmentally focused rhetoricians for overlooking systems of racial oppression in their accounts of rhetorical ecologies—a critique I take seriously. The tendency to associate racialized others with dirt or the dirty is just one of the revealing features of the hierarchies I am examining. Further, within the rhetoric of human exceptionalism, some humans are afforded full humanity, while others are cast as animal, inanimate, or disposable. The language of soil and dirt mirrors the discursive distinctions imposed by racialized systems. Interrogating these parallels recognizes the ways these terms have taken shape within the overlapping rhetorics of capitalism, industrialization, colonization, urbanization, and racial marginalization, so is one key to understanding the close connections between environmental degradation and racial injustice.

Indeed, the work of Indigenous authors, such as Robin Wall Kimmerer, demonstrates a radically different approach to understanding the material world:

In English, we never refer to a member of our family, or indeed to any person, as ‘it.’ That would be a profound act of disrespect. It robs a person of selfhood and kinship, reducing a person to a mere thing. So it is that in Potawatomi and most other indigenous languages, we use the same words to address the living world as we use for our family. Because they are our family. (402)

I close the film with lines from Old’s poem to open similar rhetorical possibilities. “[B]ut now I can see us all,” Olds writes, “made of the/ same basic materials/ cousins of that first exploding star.”

Viewers need not feel kinship with “dirt” or see it as a living being to realize the human-centric hierarchies at play within the everyday definitions at this play’s center. Like Kimmerer and Olds, Dirt: A Play in Composted Texts suggests that we might reimagine our language use about the natural world from the ground up.

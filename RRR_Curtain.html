A CODA: Performance and (Role)Play for Developing Writers
By Michelle La France

Artist Statement

While the co-editors of this digital project are enthusiastically “all in” on CDRP as a teaching practice, especially as it is described in the previous Act as a way to help students “inhabit,” “perform,” and thereby “discover new possibilities for rhetorical action” (Harris Ramsby), we recognize that not every writing teacher will be comfortable incorporating theater techniques or dramatic embodiment into their classrooms and assignments. Some readers may even be wondering, “But how does CDRP help me teach writing?”

If you hear yourself in this reaction, this final chapter is for you.

I have written elsewhere about my adaptation of “critical play” as a teaching strategy (see LaFrance and Hardee). In my mind, the imaginative dimensions of play refocus learning to write as an opportunity to negotiate rhetorical boundaries and navigate limits, while moving writing instruction further away from its roots in ideals of correctness, mastery of rules, and knowledge of academic conventions. My embrace of the critical play aligns with what Ames Hawkins and others have increasingly described as the “critical-creative,” a mode of inquiry that intentionally blurs the boundaries between critique and creation, imagining knowledge as emerging from affective, embodied, and aesthetic engagements, as well as more traditional empirical methods.

Play in this sense, is a tactical intervention into traditional writing pedagogies — a reorientation that brings forward students’ tacit awareness of forms, contexts, and written conventions. A playful approach to writing assignments, as this chapter will show, allows writing teachers to adopt the principles of CDRP in ways that are a little more familiar and that map closely to many of the discussions of writing and rhetoric currently active in our pedagogical conversations. I first began to consider the performative possibilities of critical play, when I encountered it in Laura Micciche’s work about feminist rhetorical theory. Micciche writes,

Play is not in excess to writing… Writing as play means that fictional elements are valid aspects of critical writing. Play involves performance, critical engagement with texts, considerable rhetorical skill, audience awareness, capacity to negotiate voice and tone, and an understanding of social relations – pragmatic, rhetorical knowledge, in other words. In addition, though, play entails wonder, curiosity, idealism, hyperbole, and imaginative leaps — an expansive horizon that purposefully exceeds predetermined limits.” (182)

Further, feminist rhetorical theory embraces critical play not as a distraction from serious work, but as one of its most effective modes. Play encourages layered reflection, what Lauren Berlant would call “juxtapolitical” thinking, where students both inhabit and critique social realities, recognizing how their own lives, and so their everyday language use, are embedded within and shaped by social structures. The personal is sometimes playfully political, so to speak.

I’d like to highlight the brief but crucial focus on performance in the quotation above. Micciche argues that forms such as parody and satire are not mere entertainments, but vital tools for feminist rhetoricians, forms that reveal the constructed, contested, and politically charged nature of language. But more than that, Micchiche’s nod reminds us that language is itself a kind of performance. Writing is more than a mirror of inner thought; via genre, tone, style, framing, and rhetorical flourish writing stages language.

“Writing is performative” Lunsford writes, asserting a foundational threshold concept in Addler-Kasner and Wardle’s Naming What We Know<. I’ll say it more simply: To write is to perform. Even when we remain seated, even when our bodies appear furiously still, our writing is worldmaking. Balancing and juggling rhetorical constituencies, we are writing the world and writing ourselves into the world. When teachers forefront the page, the screen, the sentence as stages, writing play upon those sites makes our embodied investments and the ways we imagine the world a little more visible.

Those interested in theories of performance or a deeper reckoning with a term like “performance” will be disappointed that I go no further in my definition. For me, the term highlights how language (so writing and rhetoric) does things in the world. Language is worldbuilding: It constructs knowledge, persuades, beguiles, and moves us; it imagines audiences into being. We move through the world with our language, especially our writing, writing ourselves and our ideas into the social fabric, into relationships, and into institutions. We take stances, stake our claim, cover our tracks, appear and reappear in the world. In that sense, writing is always performative—not because it is always staged or theatrical (though sometimes it is), but because it makes many things real. Writing makes things happen.

I also want to note here that Micciche does not suggest that play harnesses a single aspect of writing development — say voice, awareness of conventions, or rhetorical knowledge. Instead, her embrace of play recognizes writing as an inherently ecological endeavor. Another alignment: Since Marilyn Cooper’s early articulation of writing “ecologies,” many who teach writing have sought to adapt their approaches to foreground writing as a socially and materially situated activity. Jenny Rice notes that the concept of rhetorical ecologies, emphasizes writing as “a complex system of emergence that brings rhetorics into being” (Rice 9). Similarly playful exercises, I contend, offer students opportunities to test their tacit understandings of rhetorical ecologies, as they stretch their sense of how writing takes shape within similarly complex contextual and material networks.

Jody Shipka has been at the forefront of similar expectation-altering conversations, positing multimodal experimentation (with diverse modes, materials, and communicative contexts) and deep attention to the resulting, often unpredictable, processes. For Shipka, experimentation with modes fosters rhetorical dispositions helpful for today’s student writers who face a constantly changing landscape of digital tools, literacy tasks, and communicative possibilities.

Our pedagogy (and to some extent CDRP) is deeply indebted to Shipka’s. When playful curricula invite students to make purposeful choices from a range of options or when students are asked to articulate the rhetorical, technological, and material reasoning behind those choices, students “learn by doing,” Shipka argues (Task-Based 291). Improvisational play within a complex ecology becomes a method for attuning to the unpredictable emergence of discourse, shaping not only what a writer can do, but the contextual and linguistic agencies they might develop. Work with CDRP loops in additional rhetorical layers such as those highlighted in our digital acts: the body, the more than human world, space, time, rhythm, and other materialities–to what Shipka has called “transmodalites,” connecting these materialities and embodiment through drama and performance.

A compelling theoretical parallel can be found in Kenneth Burke’s dramatistic theory of rhetoric, especially Burke’s Pentad, whereby rhetorical action takes material form through the dynamic interplay of act, agent, agency, scene, and purpose. Our emphasis on performance and embodiment similarly resonates with the recent interest in the threshold concepts of Writing Studies, particularly those outlined in Naming What We Know, edited by Linda Adler-Kassner and Elizabeth Wardle. Directly relevant threshold concepts include that “writing is performative” (Lunsford) and “embodied cognition” (Bazerman and Tinberg). Additionally, “writing enacts and creates identities and ideologies” (Scott) and “writing is a representation of ideologies and identities” (Downs & Wardle). Taken together, CDRP and its cousin, critical play, are powerful modes of exploring these threshold concepts in classrooms, allowing students to not only engage with, but act out –in person or via the page–the conventions, strategies, and assumptions that inform these core concepts about writing and writers.

Many who teach writing already design writing assignments and classrooms as spaces where the creative and the critical are interwoven. We create classroom spaces based >in play> because we understand that performance-based and imaginative engagement with writing makes abstract rhetorical concepts tangible. Writing assignments that embrace critical play offer many of the same affordances as theatrical exercises: just as Augusto Boal envisioned “image-play” as a method for transforming passive viewers into active “Spect-Actors,” playful writing assignments position students to move with a previously unrecognized sense of rhetorical agency. When students are encouraged to see themselves as capable of shifting positions, adapting voices, composing with intention, imagining real-world audiences and experimenting with everyday, disciplinary, or hybrid genres, they are more likely to develop a nuanced and embodied awareness of rhetorical practice. We are delighted to contribute to this growing toolbox for writing assignments that engage critical language awareness in fresh ways.

In what follows, I explore three writing assignments from my teaching practice that take up key aspects of CDRP while grounding their approaches to performance in more conventional understandings of voice and genre. I do not mean to suggest that these assignments are earth shaking or even that unusual. (In fact, that the first two feel somewhat conventional is exactly my point.) With a few minor shifts, most of us can add a little more play to our courses or clarify the rationales that have brought us intuitively to teaching strategies that lead students toward more linguistic awareness. Not a big leap for those new to these concepts and a gentle “we see you” for those who are already enacting forms of or close cousins to CDRP and critical play.

The first assignment invites students to role-play as letter writers and then reflect on their use of “voice” in composing. The second (adapted from an assignment originally shared with me by Tyler Martinez) asks students to craft a humorous “Squib” modeled after pieces from The Onion or McSweeney’s, adopting explicit strategies, such as satire and parody. The final assignment, a “Choose Your Own Adventure” exercise, encourages students to experiment with characters/persona and genres as they blend academic and popular forms.

In each assignment, students are given explicit permission to subvert academic norms, to stretch their own voices and visions of themselves as writers in academic contexts, and to write from a place of creative knowing rather than correctness. When coupled with a final written reflection, these deliverables ask students into a meta-rhetorical space, where they might not only reflect on their purposeful choices, strategies and processes, but consider how their experiences as writers reflect what they think genres, audiences, and language should look like in a school setting. These reflections often surface the discomfort and pleasure of rhetorical experimentation, narratives which themselves make room for further insight and self-understanding.

All told, our work with the performative aspects of writing—whether explicitly rooted in or more loosely aligned with the CDRP models we’ve developed as scholars—asks teachers and students to take creative risks, to cultivate fresh notions of rhetorical agency, and to capitalize on the linguistic and rhetorical knowledge that shapes our everyday experiences.

Did we mention that the approaches I share here are also joyful? On the occasions where students might read aloud or “Show and Tell” their work, I often find myself laughing along with students, loving it when everyone leaves with a lifted mood. These assignments are simply refreshing within my teaching repertoire, enlivening classroom community, rekindling student engagement, and reintroducing a playful humanity into the often-stultifying processes of higher education. When students buy in, their work is not only rhetorically rich but also a genuine pleasure to read. Along the way, I am often surprised and delighted by the creative-critical questions, brainstorming, and drafting students share with me.

Role Play as Rhetorical Teacher: Letter Writing

A small selection of research highlights the power of role play for writers. Role play has been shown to support student argumentation (Jamaludin, Caroline, & San), foster peer-to-peer learning in writing centers (LeCluyse), and deepen student understanding of others through imaginative inhabitation (Black). A growing body of scholarship, particularly in feminist, queer, creative, and embodied pedagogies, also emphasizes the value of imaginative and performative practice (Stenberg, LaFrance and Hardee). Meanwhile, creative writing pedagogies have long embraced persona work and dramatic monologue as core strategies (Leahy and Mayers). A brief survey of open-access teaching blogs and K–12 curricula confirms role play’s ubiquity across educational contexts. And is it any wonder? As our introduction contends, techniques that allow for imaginative and felt-sense practice open the door to deeper realizations and grounded understandings.

A fellow graduate instructor first introduced me to the use of role play in the writing classroom many years ago. She asked her students to assume the voices of authors we had read, composing “fan” letters addressed to other figures on the syllabus. The year was 2001. Imagine Dr. Jane Tompkins, author of “Indians, Textualism, Morality, and the Problem of History,” writing a precocious letter to Mr. Walker Percy about his eponymous essay, “The Loss of the Creature.” Imagine 20 earnest undergrads posing questions in a voice pitched toward the erudite, rhetorically coded with twenty-something naiveté:

“Can we ever truly know the creature, Walker?”

“If we cannot see the Grand Canyon (or anything else) without the mediating frame of our prior expectations, can we ever access ‘what really happened’ without passing through layers of textual representation? Are we really always embedded in interpretive structures?”

“So, if true experience emerges when one steps outside of institutional or cultural scripts. If we cannot escape mediation, can’t we at least be more ethically aware of it? Would you agree that it’s just a matter of reframing?”

Many readers of our collection will have seen similar assignments and activities in the form of guided practices that ask students to compose while taking on a voice, persona, or unusual purpose. These strategies sound gimmicky, but many teachers will vouch that they invite rhetorical reflections in important ways. Students who struggled with finding a voice or an entrance point for a research undertaking in traditional academic essays often demonstrate a fluency when allowed to take up more familiar forms or performances.

In my classrooms, I often ask students to adopt an author’s voice and perspective as they compose a letter or series of letters between authors or public figures. My most current role play assignment (see example 1 in the Appendix) invites students to inhabit the voice of Amy Robillard, the author of “Searching for Stories.” In this piece, Robillard narrates her family’s relationship to stories (they were not storytellers, Robillard notes), pausing at key moments of realization to connect to other academic thinkers. “One way to think about the work we do in rhetoric and composition is exactly this: bringing to conscious attention our selection/evaluation systems,” Robillard writes in one location (20). In another location she writes, “We seek ways to identify with the stories we find so that we can feel a sense of belonging to a tribe” (23), a quotation that explains the work of role play, as well.

Robillard’s penultimate riff on Althusser’s concept of interpellation seems a direct indicator of why so many writing teachers find role play a useful tool for learners. In culture, so in storytelling and critical play, “characters are hailed, or interpolated,” Robillard paraphrases Althusser. Stories ask us to “identify” with, as, and in relation to others (28), Robillard explains. Roleplay asks for a similar process of identification.

The epistolary form further asks students to rhetorically engage with concepts such as tone, persona, and audience, using more familiar and informal explanatory markers than most academic reading reflections or writing exercises. Robillard emphasizes the importance of rhetorical empathy and perspective-taking, and I encourage students to slip Robillard’s distinctive voice on like a “guise,” while articulating her ideas about reframing writing research as listening–a process of piecing together stories.

Ultimately, role play and letter writing encourage students to see rhetoric as a living, flexible, and deeply personal practice. When we try on roles, we are (implicitly or explicitly) interrogating identity and power, even if not always overtly cultivating a sense of how rhetoric works for us. The exercise allows us to talk about the choices students must make, however. And that puts us in a position to take up the complexities of voice and mimicry, and to think with Robillard a little more. Would she agree with your use of her voice? What do you think she would say about how you used that concept? From this initial starting point, I am able to return students to readings and concepts we have taken up previously and even to reflect how often writers are asked to write in voices that are not their own.

In my experience, giving students a character, role, or exaggerated persona is often a key to unlocking a different relationship to writing. A student playing the role of an overzealous English Professor grammar-policing fliers in a hallway must negotiate ethos as a performance, steering their writing between caricature, contradiction, absurdity or meaningfulness. A student writing as the jaded attendant at the campus center on a snow day learns to mimic the register of official (or unofficial) communications. But, too, to write in character requires that we simultaneously work through the mutually informing rhetorical webs of genre, tone, and purpose. Through these acts of writing-as-performance, students are asked to consider how their ideals of audience, rhetorical constraints, and purposes might come together.

The “Squib”: Embracing Satire and Parody

Inspired by the short daily humor articles found in The Onion, McSweeney’s, and The Daily Squib (a UK site similar to The Onion), this “Squib” assignment asks students to write short satirical or humorous and creative responses inspired by their experiences in the real world. I was initially introduced to this assignment by a PhD student at George Mason, Dr. Tyler Martinez, who had been a content creator for a number of different web-based companies before becoming a student in our program. (Anyone who knows Tyler well knows that he has a keen, often quiet, but wry, sense of humor, largely inspired by his Cajon upbringing.)

Students might find inspiration for their weekly Squib while standing in line at the crowded student center or exploit an interesting point in course readings or class discussions. Experiences in a student club, as a commuter in a car-centric county just outside the nation’s capital, or as a consumer of culture are all fair game for satirical exploitation. My only rule, I often remind students, is that they cannot attack, bully, or demean another person, especially someone in the class, our larger campus community, or that we may know in common.

Otherwise, they may write as themselves; they may take on an imagined persona; or they may write in the voice of a public figure. As they experiment with tone and genre, they must shoot to make their classmates and I laugh—infusing their writing with wit, jokes, the ribald, the ironic, and the lowest hanging fruit of social critique.

As an instructor, I truly enjoy the freedom and playfulness of the Squib. Student versions of the assignment might put quotations from the class readings into conversation with the day’s news or a movie trailer that just dropped. They might take any one of a dozen popular forms: a script, short story, news article, man-on-the street interaction, letter, ballad, podcast, YouTube commercial, Tiktok video, recipe, song, online catalog description, UrbanDictionary definition, poem, or other inventive mode. Students take turns reading their Squibs aloud in class and receive immediate feedback on voice, style, and genre awareness. It’s our own little version of The Daily Show, only we don’t have to wait up so late for the episodes to drop. (If the sheer freedom and the shortness of the form do not sell you–I score these with credit/no credit, both increasing the opportunity for students to risk themselves and experiment without fear of failure.)

I’d love to say my choice to bring satire into my course was backed by research—in truth, encouraging students to write satirically has felt more like a survival strategy in a world that has made this teacher of many, many years feel a number of big, difficult emotions. There is, of course, plenty of research about satire as a genre (“the mirror of the world” as one blogger calls it [Emil]), but the research on satire as a teaching tool is sparse. This may reflect the difficulties of employing humor as a teaching tool—one person’s satire is another person’s pain point, after all. Yet, when thoughtfully applied, research has shown that humor is a fantastic addition to our teaching, facilitating opportunities for meaningful classroom engagement, critical reflection, and strong classroom rapport (Ellingson 123). The “affective dimension” (Hendricks) of this type of writing is yet another tie between the performing writer and CDRP.

My untested experience is that these performative assignments grant student writers a type of rhetorical agency not available to them in more traditional academic forms. As Kristen McDermott argues, writing an effective satire “demands careful cognitive work and contextualization” (p. 349). An author of satire must not only critically engage with social and cultural phenomena, but perform via exaggeration and attention to registers, conventions, and other norms that guide textual production. Parody, in particular, may be understood to help students understand the nuances of voice, as effective mimicry requires attunement to language practices, content, audience, and style.

Another key reason I enjoy this assignment is that the Squib’s form recognizes the discourse communities to which students already belong—they can write as experts about familiar bodies of knowledge. Today’s students are frequently readers of and ready-made audiences for similar genres, just as they are the experts of their own lives, the cultural forms and messages they engage with daily, and the rhetorical strategies that resonate within their communities. Familiarity empowers them to write with greater confidence, purpose, and authenticity, bridging the gap between academic expectations and lived experience. As McDermott argues,

That our students are self-aware, sophisticated consumers of [humorous] media is, of course, no secret. But by presenting material they identify with culturally, by providing them with accessible, focused theoretical essays… and by giving them frequent opportunities to seek out examples on their own, students of comedy actively embrace critical analysis for its own sake. (p. 350)

Recognition of irony, contradiction, or absurdity can indeed be a powerful and compelling form of critique—one that invites reflection, challenges dominant narratives, and provokes meaningful dialogue with audiences both familiar and resistant. As an entrance point into further rhetorical work, the Squib assignment allows students to develop their rhetorical sensibilities through performing genres and their social critiques in low-stakes yet intellectually rich ways.

Choose Your Own Adventure: Playing within Imagined Ecologies

The final assignment I’ll discuss in this chapter asks students to select from a variety of genre-based personas. They are then tasked with producing a short series of public-facing works rooted in the academic themes, texts, and debates of the course. I’ve called this a “Choose Your Own Adventure” assignment, because it prompts students to select their approach from a prewritten menu of persona-based scenarios (and closely related hybrid genres). (A fellow writing teacher once asked me whether all writing wasn’t always “Choose Your Own Adventure,” but I will reserve my reply for another essay.)

As I designed this assignment, I wanted an approach that invited risk-taking and improvisation, allowing student writers to feel the stakes of rhetorical decision-making rather than merely analyze them, thus transforming rhetorical flexibility into an embodied, reflexive practice. To enable this, the personas students might choose from were genre-based: Podcast producer, video creator, social media influencer, longform essayist, blogger, or academic news writer (see the Appendix for the assignment text). Each option asked students to both enact their persona and take up key ideas from course readings and discussions, thereby experimenting with the hybridity of genre, voice, and medium.

My thinking was that exploring academic knowledge-making and signaling their growing expertise in unusual ways made the familiar processes of writing strange. Whether composing a blog series, producing a podcast, or crafting a satirical video channel, students were also encouraged to make their work public and shareable via web-based platforms and class discussion boards. A major component of our coursework together was a weekly “show and tell,” where students would share their “deliverables” and garner further discussion of the readings and shared work of the class through their engagement with course readings and themes.

At its core, the assignment is grounded in the belief that rhetorical and critical language awareness come to the fore when students are tasked with unusual and playful writing tasks. Assuming roles, say the “GRRL Friday News Writer” or the “Hottest Social Media Influencer,” required students to try on the rhetorical and sociopolitical positions suggested by personas, while reflecting playfully on tone, ethos, medium, and cultural forms. As I’ve stated above, I believe that this kind of performative work opens space for students to understand discourse not just as something to critique, but as something we co-constitute. A key tenet of Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA) is that discourse is always contextual and negotiated. Our roles, positions, and experiences position us, but through those unfolding standpoints, we participate in writing the world.

I have been pleased to see the majority of students positioning themselves as actively aware language users, sidestepping typical institutional expectations for their writing to find creative purchase as they carried out their work. One notable student, (now) Dr. Jay Hardee, self-styled himself the “Irreverent Rhetorician” in his responses to this assignment, creating a video series that blended queer theory with irreverent, queer commentary (see LaFrance and Hardee for a discussion of this student’s work–it was so very cool we were moved to write about it together). Jay surpassed my expectations for student responses to this assignment, tackling topics like fetish porn (yes, I know–edgy, isn’t it?), the ethical rhetorics of the Stonewall Riots, Youtubing bisexual Barbies, and “gay-sounding” voices. Each video is exhaustively researched, grounded in relevant queer theory, and composed with a sense of style writers often work for years to achieve. Jay adapts his performance style to the messages he offers, one moment delivering short, witty riffs on rhetorical concepts, classroom practices, or the absurdities of academic life, the other theatrically sending up some aspect of queer culture or his life as a gay dad.

The Chose You Own Adventure series offered a fantastic experience for PhD-level writers, providing a liberating space for writers like Jay to explore provocative content while developing an authoritative scholarly voice that personalized the degree of playfully disruptive cache they adopted. More than just fun, however, the project was transformative for several students—an invitation to abandon rigid academic norms and embrace creative play as a powerful mode that remade academic expression. I’ve had similar success using the form in undergraduate courses, as well.

While Jay has been the only student to take my invitation to write about the experience of completing this assignment, others also pushed the envelope of their own writing, creating blogs that explored the personal connections between our readings and parenting, podcasts about writing and wellness, scripts for a one person theatrical show about the absurdities of teaching, and even a Dungeons & Dragons campaign modeled after The Odyssey (with Writing Studies luminaries stationed around a campus map). Embracing the spirit of critical play and playing with language via their writing, these students showed me the power of assignments that ask for students to creatively perform their writing. They did not simply infuse their reading responses and critical arguments with personal insights about how this genre-play expanded their sense of themselves as critical language users and writers, they embraced the deep rhetorical learning offered by adopting humor, creative remixing, and theatricality. The result was an engaging multimedia collection of thoughtful, imaginative, and deeply engaging student work.

Conclusion

A fascinating note of synchronicity. As I was completing this chapter, I received a marketing email from Caleb Madison, Games Director at The Atlantic, who writes about the power of games—and so play

Excavations in Mesopotamia and Egypt uncovered ancient games such as the Royal Game of Ur and Hounds and Jackals. With no inkling of an alphabet or the marketplace, people still sat across from each other with a board between them, rolling dice, moving tokens, following rules. It’s historical proof that channeling the competitive spirit into structured interaction with invented stakes is more than just a distraction. It’s a ritual foundational to the human experience.

This unexpected email felt like a fitting coda to the work I’ve traced in this chapter and that we’ve developed in this digital collection. It’s a reminder that play and performance are not only deeply impactful meaning-making tools in writing classrooms, but that these experiences speak to us on a deeply human level.

We have argued throughout this digital collection for the value of theories and pedagogies that allow students to see how language and power coincide. The types of assignments we have developed–exercises and explorations of grammatical principles, metaphors, and other linguistic mechanics–function as deliberate, theoretically informed pedagogies that can illuminate the tacit and sometimes mystifying conventions of academic and everyday discourse, as they foreground student embodiments and performative-knowledge.

Play via games may have once superseded the need for alphabetic knowledge, but for writing teachers seeking to showcase the performative nature of language in their classrooms and assignments, activities that reanimate “rules” offer powerful ways to illuminate the very dynamics of discourse. By engaging in critical play and performance in writing assignments, students might step outside of themselves into experimentation; humans do not play for plays’ sake alone, play is often what Anthropologist Clifford Geertz famously called “deep,” its stakes are social, so ethical, political, and rhetorical. The writing classroom and many of our assignments are ready to be remade through these principles.

When forms of critical play become an epistemological mode—a method for learning that invites and recognizes performance–projects ask students to experience the doing of discourse: to inhabit rhetorical situations, announce the pressures they sense and surf, to experiment with strategy. Through improvisation and iteration, students discover how discourse norms work to authorize or marginalize certain voices and ways of speaking. They rehearse, revise, and perform discourse in ways that require them to notice more attentively how writers negotiate the spoken and unspoken linguistic rules within systems of power. In so doing, they enact the very principles of CDA central to our project and are set up to intervene powerfully in the public discourses that order their own lives.

Appendix: Assignments 1, 2, and 3

Assignment 1: Role Play

For this exercise, write a letter in the voice of Amy Robillard, author of “Searching for Stories.” That is, imagine that Robillard is a character that you can slip into–let your words embody her voice, try to write from her perspective, write with the words you believe she would use.

Your audience is a curious undergraduate student who has just read her essay. The student is confused but intrigued—what does Robillard mean by this “search?” How might Robillard suggest that we carry out this search?

Details: Your letter should be 250 to 300 words long—andin the voice of Amy Robillard, using first person. In your letter, "Amy" should offer at least two quotations from her essay and dig into other details to explain one or more of the following:

  • Why does she title the piece “Searching for Stories?”
  • What values or principles underlie her search—especially regarding listening, noticing, and reframing assumptions?
  • Are storiesonlyevidence? What other rhetorical work do stories do?

Assignment 2: Five “Squibs”

(Many thanks again to Dr. Tyler Martinez for introducing me to this assignment. This version is substantially adapted for use in a 300-level class on rhetorical understandings of “the essay”)

A squib is a short, satirical or humorous piece of “news” writing. The squib you will write will be no longer than 400 words.

This semester, I ask you to write five squibs—by that I mean, one page “light” responses to the readings, the class, our conversations, your life, your life as a student at George Mason University, your life as a student at George Mason University right now in a tense election year with the world literally on fire much of the time and somehow—What?Why?—despite it being the end of the world, we all still have to go to work every day and pay our rent.

Here are some sites where you will find example Squibs:

  • The Onion: https://theonion.com/
  • The Daily Squib: https://www.dailysquib.co.uk/
  • McSweeney’s Internet Tendency: https://www.mcsweeneys.net/

Some tips:

  • The soul of the Squib is satire. There are a lot of examples online of great satire. You’ll know it when you see it.
  • Really, really, really try to be focused on some aspect of the readings (or secondarily the class).
  • Cite from the readings in your Squib and I promise you I will perform the most excited teacher reactions each time. (You actually READ the article for this week?! You are so, so smart and clearly the smartest, kindest, and most earnest student I have EVER HAD!”)
  • Title your Squib. Yes; like a headline or a poem or an essay.
  • Please be funny! You can also be vulgar, humorous, satirical, political, a little mean, not too mean, and certainly not THAT mean. (Or, that is, being mean to someone we like [like ME] can backfire and just come off as. . . justmean.) Sadly,mean-for-mean’s sake is not really that funny, so make sure your version of mean is truly necessary social commentary over regular “I’m cranky-meanness.” The line there will be moving constantly.
  • You can also just be “reflective,” if you like. Think “influencer,” “star reporter,” or “cool podcaster” as persona, if funny, satirical, or other voices/styles/personae don’t feel quite right.
  • Play with form, as well. It doesn’t have to be a news article each time—maybe it’s a letter or a personaladd or a ballad or a Viking Mead Hall Boast or a play.

Assignment 3: Choose Your Own Adventure Assignment

Game ON!: What adventure (persona) do you choose this summer? Choose from one of the following. Though your work may take on many different shapes/actions/qualities this semester, please be sure that the readings, materials, and discussions of the course remain the root content for what you produce. I want to discourage any “pure” reflection or traditional “responses.” That is, what you produce should (creatively) take up and explore the ideas and conversations presented in our readings and class sessions. I encourage fun, creativity, character play, and VISION, even as I ask you to offer audience- and genre-appropriate attention to scholarly ideals in your work.

Vid-Cast Master: Get your 21st c School House Tiger King Rock on! With this adventure you will create 4 videos (of at least five minutes). Like any self-respecting YouTube star, you may even want your own online channel. Be sure to compose your vid-casts in ways that change up, model, extend, or challenge the many different genres of vid-play for educational and infotainment purposes.

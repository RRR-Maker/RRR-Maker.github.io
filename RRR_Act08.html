Artist Statement

Rhetorical, CDA, and Theater Connections:

  • Rhetorical framing: Making the invisible visible in political discourses; Critical Language & Drama Based Pedagogy
  • CDA Connection: Conceptual metaphor analysis, assumptive presuppositions, and nominalizations
  • Boal’s connection: Forum & Image Theater

As a newly minted rhetoric PhD travelling the job circuit, my favourite part of the teaching demonstration was showing students how theatrical play can demystify grammar rules. My biggest hit was having volunteers act out the difference between the active voice construction, “Dr. Frankenstein added the evil solution to the bubbling mixture,” then switch it up and perform, “The evil solution was added to the bubbling mixture by Dr. Frankenstein." Somehow wicked liquids and mad scientists appealed to first year writers, whose eyes often glazed over at anything resembling grammar instruction. When students performed “The evil solution was added to the bubbling mixture” without Dr. Frankenstein, they readily understood that the sentence made complete sense without an agent doing the adding. I realized I was on to something, especially when we discussed how this variation of passive voice comes in awfully handy when one wants to relinquish responsibility for completing an action.

This demonstration emerged from my dissertation and subsequent book, which explored how brilliant 20th century American playwrights like Tony Kushner and Suzan Lori Parks have been performing critical discourse analysis (CDA) for decades ( Language and Power on the Rhetorical Stage ). This led me to explore how theater might further illuminate the work of critical discourse analysts, and Critical Discursive Rhetorical Performance (CDRP) took root.

The video performances in this Act focus on potentially manipulative linguistic structures in public and political discourse. However, inspired by Bridget Giger Lee and Katie Dawson's drama-based pedagogy (DBP) my goal here is explicitly pedagogical. As DBP demonstrates, drama can "introduce drama into educational settings that traditionally lack theatrical elements, from kindergarten to college, STEM to History." The performances in this Act illustrate this pedagogical potential for CDA.

As with much of this book, I look to Aristophanes, and Boal's “Forum Theater” for inspiration. As Michelle and I note in the introduction, Aristophanes’ characters regularly embody and perform commonplace expressions, tacitly manipulative language, and such (Major 87). Accordingly, the actors in my accompanying videos  inhabit dodgy language selections to demonstrate what the language is doing. Moreover, we might recall that Forum Theater offers actor/active audiences theatrical strategies to overcome oppression, to rehearse social change. Specifically, then, this Act channels two facets of Forum Theater: “Making the Body Expressive” and “Image Theater” ( Theater of the Oppressed  102). In “Making the Body Expressive,” Boal writes that audience participants play “a series of games by which one begins to express one’s self through the body, abandoning other, more common and habitual forms of expression” (102). Additionally, “Image Theater” allows spectators - here, students - to "intervene" by reshaping theirs and other actors' bodies to speak through new images.

Warming Up

Before exploring CDRP, students can loosen up with theatrical warm-up games that, as Boal suggests, make "the body expressive." Games like "Zip Zap Zop," my variation of “Image Theater,” and “Living Stories” prepare students for the work ahead. I also developed "Status Walks," a game that helps students explore how body positioning and spatial exploration demonstrates power dynamics.

Dawson’s Drama Based Pedagogyweb page explains that,

Zip, Zap, Zop is about focus and energy. As students pass the energy across the circle (in the form of a Zip, a Zap, or a Zop), they make eye contact with the person they send the energy to, and work together to keep the rhythm going. The activity also provides an opportunity to explore pace, specificity of choice, “energy” and sequence.

Zip, Zap, Zop allows participants to focus, listen, and work as a team. Once students are warmed up with “Zip, Zap, Zop,” we can then move on to exploring levels of embodiment, a precursor of “Image Theater.” For instance, an instructor can ask participants to reach out a hand, or a leg, or make a small shape. Then, they explore levels of that shape, through small, medium, and large movements.  For instance, for a large movement,  they reach or extend hands and legs as far as they possibly can. Tell them to stand on their tippy toes, and then crunch up into a ball on the floor. They can reach over sideways, jump, balance, and kick (ensuring that their peers are not in the way). In short, they should incrementally explore the range and variety of shapes their bodies can make.

Next, I suggest moving on to “Living Stories.” Students collect into even numbered groups. They then split that group up into 2 more. Group A tells a story using one word each and they keep going until the story comes to some sort of conclusion. Group B silently improvises whatever ridiculousness emerges from the one word story group. To start the ball rolling, have students suggest scenarios for stories. These can focus on situations as mundane as trips to the dentist, walking the dog, or picking through fruits and veggies in grocery aisles. But, it helps to introduce something wacky to the situation, so that the physical storytellers can indulge as much physical expression as possible - someone finds a banana slug in the fresh fruit aisle, perhaps. (This was a suggestion from a 6th grader I taught recently, at an acting conservatory camp). The point with the one word story is that students can’t spend too much time trying to overthink their storytelling or physical expression. They just do or say what pops into their heads. Through “Living Stories,” students learn to "read" and create meaning with their bodies, essential preparation for CDRP's work of using physical composition to analyze how powerful discourses function at micro levels.

Finally in “Status Walks,” students explore different power positions, embodying distinct roles that suggest hierarchies, such as CEO, intern, professor, or student. In my experience, once they've played “Zip Zap Zop” and “Living Stories,” they have fewer inhibitions to move about a designated space, adjusting their bodies in ways that reflect varying status levels, and considering how each position affects their pace, posture, and the amount of space they occupy. This is where I also introduce them to how much power they have depending on where they might be positioned in relation to an audience. We discuss how stage positioning itself carries meaning—how moving upstage (away from the audience) can diminish presence while downstage movement commands attention. I have them experiment with these positions: the CEO naturally gravitates toward center stage and downstage right, claiming the most powerful theatrical real estate, while the intern might find themselves pushed upstage left, literally marginalized. After these walks, the group can discuss how power dynamics manifest through levels and spatial relationships.

Sample CDRP Work

Now onto the specifics of this Act: In the sections that follow, along with their accompanying videos, I offer demonstrations of how students might use theatrical means to embody and understand the embodied mechanics and effects of 3 specific CDA tools: political metaphors , ideologically charged presuppositions , and agent-deleting nominalizations . I have found that these three are easily accessible introductions to CDRP because they lend themselves so easily to embodiment. Consequently, collaborating with a group of wonderful actors from The Spot Theater Company in Ketchum, Idaho – Brett Moellenberg, Yanna Luntz, Kathy “K.O” Oglivie, and mother/daughter team Sara and Idabelle Gorby – we brainstormed brief skits that show students how to render these abstract linguistic concepts physically tangible and emotionally resonant.

Metaphors, presupposition triggers and nominalizations are just three linguistic patterns educators and students can explore through theatrical play. Indeed, I offer further CDA tools in the appendix to this Act, which are also ripe for corporeal investigation. It is my hope that these videos inspire others to create their own dramatic ways of performing obfuscating language, so that students gain visceral comprehension of how subtly coercive discourses operate on bodies, emotions, and spatial relationships.

Embodying Political Metaphors

Conceptual metaphor analysis – a subset of critical linguistics – demonstrates how we use metaphor to construct everyday realities - including our social and political realities. When we describe a nation as a "family" or a political debate as a "war," these metaphors shape how we think about these concepts, and they can influence our actions. Often, students don't realize this, so they're surprised and gain new insight into what metaphorical language does when we embody it.

But how does conceptual metaphor analysis work? Paul Simpson, Andrea Mayr and Simon Stratham explain it this way: “We understand one concept in terms of another, through a process that involves a transference or mapping between the two concepts” (45). They go on to say, “The target domain is the topic or concept that you want to describe through the metaphor, while the source domain refers to the concept that you draw upon in order to create the metaphorical construction” (45). A common example is “LIFE [target domain] is a JOURNEY [source domain]” which we demonstrate in metaphorical expressions like “‘He has no direction in life’, ‘We are at a crossroads’, [and] ‘She’ll certainly go places’” (45).

Politicians often rely on metaphors to further their political agenda. Jonathan Charteris-Black's 2004 Corpus Approaches to Critical Metaphor Analysis shows us how political speech coalesced around several conceptual metaphors that dominated British politics from the 1970s to the 1990s: politics as conflict, sacred mission, building/construction, journey , and economic/market metaphors (Charteris-Black 65-85). I chose Charteris-Black’s 2004 corpus because of how long these metaphors have been in circulation; indeed, we might recognize how politicians, across party lines, use these metaphorical expressions today, both in Britain and the United States. Rhetorically, these metaphors are extremely persuasive, and can perpetuate policy myths.

The video titled "Performing Political Battle Metaphors" briefly demonstrates the conceptual metaphor Politics is Conflict . The metaphorical expressions that emerge from this might include:

  • Politicians "attack" opponents' positions
  • They "defend" their policies
  • They "win" or "lose" debates
  • They launch "campaigns"

To enact this metaphor, Sara and Idabelle adopt stances that embody the hierarchical variations its iterations can express. For instance, to indicate a more powerful position, Sara is elevated on a small platform at the front of the stage, and, rather comically, punches and kicks the air. Because Sara enacts a defensive stance, Idabelle cowers beneath her using a stool as a shield.

  • Sara declares, "Me and the people who back me, well, we are fighting against  your proposal."
  • Idabelle responds, "I'm protecting  what matters most."

"Fighting" and "protecting" are metaphorical expressions that manifest the deeper conceptual mapping that politics is conflict . T he actors’ positioning suggests that political engagement must  involve winners and losers, attackers and defenders.

Charteris-Black's other discoveries about political metaphors can also be acted out. For instance, folks uttering variations of the politics is a journey  metaphor might use expressions like "moving the country forward" and "paths to progress." These can be simply embodied by students' confident forward movement, their eyes fixed firmly ahead, and reaching forward gestures. Something like “returning to our values,” where students reach and gaze yearningly back to a nostalgic past, indicate how backward facing directions can suggest that something is left behind or lost. The more expansive these gestures are, the more explicit the metaphor becomes, revealing its constructed nature. When students literally reach backward while saying “returning to our values,”  the physical absurdity of grasping at an invisible past makes visible how artificial the metaphor actually is – we're not literally traveling anywhere or grabbing anything tangible.

Politics as a sacred mission  metaphors also lend themselves to enactment. Common variations of the metaphor might include, "It is our moral duty to . . .," or “I am answering my calling/higher purpose in serving my constituents.” Stances can be tall or elevated as students adopt a “moral high ground.” They can enact blessing or bestowing gestures as they state, “[X] policy is our sacred duty to uphold." This physical exaggeration reveals how politicians use sacred language to make their positions seem divinely ordained rather than politically motivated.

Inhabiting these metaphors revealed their limitations to The Spot actors. In fact, Brett, one of group, observed during our exploration of political metaphors, 'It's hard to envision politics framed in other ways.” We are so used to politics being so adversarially framed, that we, and politicians, foreclose on more collaborative approaches to governance. Embodying these metaphors, and others, can show students just how pervasive and reality building they are.

Act 3

C4 Street
by Gabrielle Wilkosz

transcript

 

Artist Statement

Rhetorical, CDA, and Theater Connections:

  • Rhetorical framing: Satire as social commentary
  • CDA connection: Agent obfuscation, passive voice
  • Boal connections: Spect-Actors, Image-play: Amplifying the Body as Rhetor

My piece explores the passive (exonerating) language used in journalistic discussions of the 1985 MOVE bombing. On May 13, 1985 Philadelphia police dropped military-grade explosives on 6221 Osage Avenue, a row house belonging to MOVE, a black liberation group (DeLisle, p. iii). The police commissioner ordered fire hoses to be turned off, allowing 61 homes to be consumed by the flames (The Economist, 1985). By the end of the standoff, the Philadelphia police had used 10,000 rounds of ammunition and a military-grade satchel bomb, which was dropped from a helicopter. The resulting fire burned for 90 minutes, displacing over 300 residents as a whole residential block was consumed by flames (Stein 2022, p. 160). Heather Ann Thompson (2021) called the event “a level of trauma that is difficult to exaggerate” (The New Yorker, p. 1).

The release of Rehearse, Revise, Regenerate (2025) coincides with the 40th anniversary of the standoff. MOVE, described as a “a cult-like organization” by the Philadelphia Inquirer, was known for its anti-establishment lifestyle, including raw diets, outhouses, and visible resistance to Western norms (Abraham, 2000, p. 27). The MOVE/police conflict occurred due to what the Zinn Education Project (2022) called “nuisance reasons such as trash piled up around the home and. . . political speech blaring from a bullhorn” (p. 1). In the end, the military-grade satchel bombs dropped by police killed eleven people and displaced 61 families.

“That a city could bomb a neighborhood and burn it to the ground without repercussions is unthinkable,” said West Philadelphian and Code Switch journalist Gene Demby in a 2015 NPR interview. Decades later, he still struggles to understand what happened in his neighborhood.

Demby’s confusion around the MOVE bombing mirrors the confusion of the broader public. As the Zinn Education Project Instagram post highlights, the degree of force applied to the MOVE compound seems wildly out of balance with the conditions precipitating police intervention. While MOVE was a black liberation group, they held to a “life-centered” philosophy (PBS 2025). Notably, MOVE “is not an acronym, but an emphasis of their living philosophy”:

Each individual life is dependent on every other life, and all life has a purpose, so all living beings, things that move, are equally important, whether they are human beings, dogs, birds, fish, trees, ants, weeds, rivers, wind or rain.

Neighbors had frequently complained that MOVE played loud speakers (day and night), that adult members of the group often screamed profanities at neighbors, that they had illegally fenced off their backyard and kept a large number of dogs, slept on the roof of their row house and raw across neighbors’ rooves, and had built a bunker (MacDonald 2025). But, none of these issues demanded the type of police response that unfolded.

My goal in this project is to highlight how journalistic reporting dehumanized those killed while justifying the actions of the Philadelphia police and FBI (who supplied more than 37 pounds of military plastic explosives before the attack). The language of journalistic accounts exonerate law enforcement and city administrators. Astonishingly, The Economist (1985) took a playful tone in describing the aftermath of the standoff, comparing the act of state-sponsored violence to a “soap opera” with “sheer drama.” MOVE isn’t mentioned, and the dead are referred to as “black radicals.” Newsweek referred to the dead as “11 members of the radical group MOVE,” identifying the five dead children only as “members.” The Philadelphia Inquirer referred to the dead as simply “bodies” (Appendix B).

So, how is it possible that the “life-centered” pacifistic language of an obnoxious (airhorns blaring political speech) but well-meaning group like MOVE gets churned into sanitized news accounts? Vijith Assar’s (2015) satirical exploration of the passive voice may offer one possibility. “Facepalm Pilot: Where Technology Meets Stupidity: An Interactive Guide to Ambiguous Grammar” in McSweeney’s Internet Tendency describes how “language is an art of nuisance” (p. 1). He demonstrates how the passive voice in American Standard English obscures actors and actions with the sentence, “The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog,” which turns into “Speed was involved in a jumping‑related incident with a lazy dog while a fox was brown.”

On the surface, passive language is a common move that reflects a standard of “neutral” news reporting within journalistic accounts of everyday events. In this case, the news reporting of the MOVE bombing, especially in venues like the Philadelphia Tribune, diffuses accountability and makes it difficult to understand the trauma perpetuated by state-sanctioned violence. (I provide screenshots of two of the original news sources I satirize below in Appendices A and B.)

In my satirization of the tragedy’s news coverage, which often relied on passive sentence constructions, dehumanizing MOVE members as “black radicals” rather than women, men, and children, for instance (The Economist, 1985), I wrote several passive and active iterations of a news lede. For example, the first sentence “On May 13, 1984 syndicates were involved in a razing-related incident with a C4 bomb, sixty-one homes, and 10,000 rounds of ammunition, and five children” morphs into “And killed five children. Five of us were children.”

My characters also become “Spect-actors” (Boal) as they repeated variations of language voiced by Mayor Wilson Goode who controversially approved and defended the outsized police-response. Similar to Boal’s image-play, where living actors bring a scene to life to highlight how language is at work upon their bodies, my short video riffs on the genre of a children’s show to ironically demonstrate how journalists failed in many cases to recognize the responsibility of city officials and the humanity of the MOVE bombing victims. Much as children’s programming focuses on language learning and social skill building (like conflict resolution) repetition of short phrases within a scenario amplifies how passive, and so exonerating, language erases or recognizes actors, attributing or removing responsibility for decisions. I have named my video “C4 Street,” in homage to the most watched children’s television show in the US, Sesame Street.

Using elements of critical discourse analysis and Boal’s suggestion that the performing body can be “a site of and vehicle for the social order, with language practices as a central element in creating, sustaining, and ordering the social” (2008, p. 112), C4 Street focuses on three women who represent different forms of discourse about the event: Speaker One, who brandishes a megaphone, mimics media detachment and the distance of the political elite; Speaker Two, seated in a trashcan with trash in her hair, peeking over the edge of a trash can, performs “exonerative language” in a childlike tone; and Speaker Three, in ball cap and backpack, who exaggerates a childlike performance, to personify children, drawing attention to the five children killed in the bombing. The language these characters perform is a dissonant blend of humor, empathy, and indictment (Appendix C).

When used carefully, satire can awaken audiences to deep emotional and political stakes. My piece offers a sliver of understanding about how language may be used to provide cover for violent abuses of power.

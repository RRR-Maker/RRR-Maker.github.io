Director’s Note

  • Embodied Rhetorics: This Act turns viewers’ attention toward the media discourses through which predominantly white institutions interpreted, sanitized, and reproduced the MOVE bombing. Through a perverse take on Sesame Street, white, female actors perform these discourses in a skit that satirizes news media language.
    The satire of this Act echoes points raised in the Introduction. Chavez asks: “How does ‘the body’ matter in rhetorical studies? Which bodies matter in rhetoric?” (242). Happe argues that race is reproduced through “the ritualistic, repeated utterance of speech acts” (135).
  • Performing CDA: Listen closely to the language this Act performs. Watch for the tension between the emotional neutrality of the words and the expressive capacities of the bodies performing them. This Act rewrites found news ledes to expose how “passive verb usage, erasure of institutional actors, and euphemistic descriptors” enact the “linguistic dirty work” described in our Introduction. As words are omitted and bureaucratically framed, the video’s performance reveals how grammar attempts to subtly shape public memory. Note how the language featured in this Act focuses on obscure responsibility for racial violence while making institutional power appear neutral.
  • Boal Connection: This Act’s “image-play” stages exonerating language as a visible, bodily phenomenon. The Sesame Street-like register turns dispassionate language against itself: the genre conventions of children’s educational media collide with language that erases their violent deaths. This lands in the harshest lines, “And killed five children. Five of us were children.”

Artist Statement

On May 13, 1985, the City of Philadelphia authorized the bombing of 6221 Osage Avenue, the home of MOVE, a Black liberation and environmental justice organization. The attack lives in public memory as a profound act of state violence that devastated a residential neighborhood and left lasting trauma for Philadelphia’s Black communities. The bombing killed eleven MOVE members, including five children, and left more than 250 residents homeless after an entire city block was allowed to burn. This piece examines how journalistic language about the bombing, particularly the use of passive sentence construction, diffuses responsibility for the bombing and exonerates city officials.

Before examining the language used to describe the bombing, it is important to understand how MOVE understood itself. MOVE was a Black liberation organization organized around a “life-centered” philosophy that emphasized the interdependence of all living beings (PBS). The organization emphasized that their name was “not an acronym but an emphasis of their living philosophy”:

Each individual life is dependent on every other life, and all life has a purpose, so all living beings, things that move, are equally important, whether they are human beings, dogs, birds, fish, trees, ants, weeds, rivers, wind or rain. (PBS)

Yet the public discourse surrounding the organization rarely centered these values.

Years of neighborhood complaints, political conflict, and aggressive policing produced a news narrative in which MOVE appeared primarily as a public nuisance or threat. As the Zinn Education Project notes, many of the immediate justifications for intervention arose from disputes that would ordinarily be regarded as minor neighborhood conflicts.

By the end of the standoff, Philadelphia police had used 10,000 rounds of ammunition and a military-grade satchel bomb, which was dropped from a helicopter. The resulting fire burned for 90 minutes, displacing over 300 residents as the entire residential block was consumed by flames (Stein 160). Heather Ann Thompson called the event “a level of trauma that is difficult to exaggerate” (The New Yorker 1). “That a city could bomb a neighborhood and burn it to the ground without repercussions is unthinkable,” Gene Demby, a West Philadelphian and Code Switch journalist, said in a 2015 NPR interview. Decades later, he still struggles to understand what happened in his neighborhood.

My short video deploys examples of news agency language about the bombing to call attention to how these pieces produce public consent for racialized state violence. Through passive verb usage, erasure of institutional actors, and euphemistic descriptors, many news accounts obscured responsibility while simultaneously dehumanizing MOVE members, normalizing an extraordinary and outsized use of force against a Black community. In reports about the bombing, mainstream media outlets frequently characterized MOVE through pathologizing and sensationalized language, often describing the organization as “cult-like” while foregrounding its unconventional lifestyle practices. Such descriptions tended to obscure MOVE’s commitments to Black liberation, environmental stewardship, communal living, and resistance to state authority.

Most insidiously, the language of newspaper accounts exonerates law enforcement and city administrators. Astonishingly, The Economist took a playful tone in describing the aftermath of the standoff, comparing the act of state-sponsored violence to a “soap opera” with “sheer drama.” MOVE isn’t mentioned, and the dead are referred to as “black radicals.” Newsweek referred to the dead as “11 members of the radical group MOVE,” identifying the five dead children only as “members.” The Philadelphia Inquirer referred to the dead as simply “bodies” (Appendix B). This language functions ideologically, redirecting attention away from the officials who authorized the bombing and toward abstract descriptions of events. In doing so, discourse becomes a mechanism through which accountability is diffused and state violence is rendered both ordinary and inevitable.

How does a movement grounded in a philosophy of interconnected life become represented primarily through narratives of disorder, deviance, and threat? How do media institutions transform a community’s self-understanding into a public story that legitimizes violent state intervention? Vijith Assar’s satirical exploration of the passive voice offers one possibility. “Facepalm Pilot: Where Technology Meets Stupidity: An Interactive Guide to Ambiguous Grammar” in McSweeney’s Internet Tendency describes how “language is an art of nuisance.” He demonstrates that the passive voice in American Standard English obscures actors and actions with the sentence, “The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog,” which turns into “Speed was involved in a jumping-related incident with a lazy dog while a fox was brown.”

On the surface, passive language is a common move that reflects a standard of “neutral” news reporting within journalistic accounts of everyday events. In this case, the news reporting of the MOVE bombing, especially in venues like the Philadelphia Tribune, diffused accountability, a rhetorical strategy that makes it difficult to understand the trauma perpetuated by state-sanctioned violence. (I provide screenshots of two of the original news sources I satirize below in Appendices A and B.)

In my satirization of the tragedy’s news coverage, which often described MOVE members as “black radicals” rather than women, men, and children (The Economist), I wrote several passive and active iterations of a news lede to return attention to the results of the bombing. For example, the first sentence, “On May 13, 1984, syndicates were involved in a razing-related incident with a C4 bomb, sixty-one homes, and 10,000 rounds of ammunition, and five children,”morphs into “And killed five children. Five of us were children.”

My characters become “Spect-Actors” (Boal xxi) as they repeat variations of language voiced by Mayor Wilson Goode, who controversially approved and defended the outsized police response. Similar to Boal’s image-play, where living actors bring a scene to life to highlight how language is at work upon their bodies, my short video riffs on the genre of a children’s show to ironically demonstrate how journalists failed in many cases to recognize the humanity of the MOVE bombing victims. Much as children’s programming focuses on language learning and social skill building (like conflict resolution), repetition of short phrases within a scenario amplifies how passive and so exonerating language erases or recognizes actors, attributing or removing responsibility for decisions. I have named my video “C4 Street” in homage to the most watched children’s television show in the US, Sesame Street.

Using elements of critical discourse analysis and Boal’s suggestion that the performing body can be “a site of and vehicle for the social order, with language practices as a central element in creating, sustaining, and ordering the social” (Theatre of the Oppressed 112), C4 Street focuses on three white women who represent different forms of journalistic discourse about the event. Speaker One, who brandishes a megaphone, mimics media detachment and the distance of political elites; Speaker Two, seated in a trash can with trash in her hair, performs “exonerative language” in a childlike tone; and Speaker Three, wearing a ball cap and backpack, invokes the presence of children whose lives were lost in the bombing.

I acknowledge the dissonance created by placing white female bodies at the center of a performance critically examining and critiquing the language used to rationalize the bombing of a Black liberation organization and the deaths of eleven Black people, including five Black children. As our team of actors, all white, female educators, grappled with this particular history of anti-Black state violence and the ethical challenges of representing that history through performance, we want to underline that the dead were not abstractions or political symbols to us. They were Black parents, children, neighbors, and community members whose lives were rendered expendable by state decision-makers.

Rather than attempting to speak for MOVE members or recreate Black suffering, the performance intends to turn attention toward the discourses through which predominantly white institutions (such as news organizations and city offices) have interpreted, sanitized, and re-produced the event. The performers’ bodies thus function not as stand-ins for MOVE members but as sites through which audiences can examine the racialized assumptions, linguistic evasions, and structures of privilege that made such violence intelligible and, for many observers, acceptable. The language these characters perform is a dissonant blend of humor, empathy, self-implication, and indictment (Appendix C).

When used carefully, satire can awaken audiences to the emotional and political stakes that are underrecognized in our everyday communications. As the introduction to this collection argues, discourse operates as a technology of power, especially when it participates in processes that shape public memory and obscure accountability. By foregrounding the humanity of those killed and exposing the linguistic mechanisms through which responsibility is erased, the project invites audiences to reconsider not only the MOVE bombing itself but also our own relationships to language, race, state violence, and collective memory.
